TO ARMS, TO ARMS, YE BONNY HIGHLAND LADDIES [1]


1. To Arms, to Arms, ye Bonny Highland Laddies,
To Arms to Arms at the beat of the Drum!
The French Horns [2] sound! Put on the Traws and Plaidy,
For Charly, our Royall Prince Regent is come.

There's never a Clan that Trips it o'er the Plain
But will Truckle to the Man that is five Foot Ten
And the Tune that they strike on the Tabor and the Pipe
Is "The King shall enjoy his own Again".

2. Britannia's Chief, all Europe shall Caress Thee
And drink a health to thy speedy Return.
Of Charly, I mean, the son of Sobiesky [3].
The Beautious Flower of Edenbourgh's Come.

Then Toss off the Bowls, the Brave and Jolly S(k)oals,
In a health to the Gallant Queen of Spain! [4]
To Philip, lead on! and Charly her Son! [5]
And the King shall enjoy his own Again.

3. Brave Fitz-James, the Gallant Son of Flander [6]
He comes to our Aid with a Jovial Crew,
Stout as Alexander he fills the World with Wonder
And Sails by the Advice of Brave Richlieu. [7]

Let not the Courage fail, the fleet is under Sail
With O'Neal Gordon [8] and the brave Lord John, [9]
For Lewis Le Grand has given his Command [10]
That the King shall enjoy his own Again.

4. At Preston Pans, A very Pretty Story,
A smarter Engagement there never was Seen,
The Lawland Lads instead of win(n)ing Glory
Fled whilst their Troops lay sprawling on the Green.

That Cowardly Cope being Destitute of Hope
Did suddenly Elope and forsake his Men
For fly he must, he knew it was but Just
That the King should enjoy his own Again.

5. At Faulkirk Moor we met with Bully Hawley
Commanding the Left and Flask on the Right.
He Curssed the Boars who led him to the Folly
To Tempt against Jove and his King for to fight.

Our pieces being large, we such Volleis did Discharge
And our Bullets did descend like a shower of Rain.
Then swift as the Wind they fled and left their Baggage all Behind
That the King might enjoy his own Again.

6. Hoist your Canoes and Puppies and get Over
No more of your Schemes of curssed Old Noll!
Let Prusia King be Possess'd of Hanover
And Feather his Nest with Bohemia's Moll! [11]

A Badge of Renown, A Coffin or A Crown
We will Conquer or we'll Dye for the best of Men.
To Willy and to George give a total Discharge
And the King shall enjoy his own Again.

7. But Oh the Fatal Battle of Coloden
Has totally Eclipst all our Glory and Renown.
Our late Conquering Troops they under Foot was (sic) Troden
And Charlie, Disapoint'd in the hopes of A Crown.

Then talk no more of Cope nor how Hawley did elope,
Nor let us Boast again of our Broad Sword and Shield
But let the Prince go Home and  tell his Friends at Rome
THAT GEORG STILL WILL CONQUER AND REBELLION MUST YIELD!

*************************
[1] This Whig song is a parody of the Jacobite song [e10] "To your arms, my bonnie Highland lads" published by James Hogg in "Jacobite Relics, Vol. II", P. 129, song LXV (1821).
Stanzas 1 and 4 paraphrase stanzas 1 and 3 of the original, respectively, in a sarcastic manner.
The rest is a false eulogy of the 1745 uprising and concludes by condemning it.
In spite of the Scotticizing words it contains ("bonny", "laddies", "traws", "plaidy"...) this son is clearly English, unlike its model.
Hoggs states that he learnt the tune to the original song from the singing of a named Lizzy Lamb, cottager at Ladhope but supposed, judging but the final burden of each stanza, that it must have been composed to [A16] "The king shall enjoy his own again".

[2] The satiric purpose of the pastiche seems to expose Prince Charles Stuart as a foreign agent and his followers as his docile accomplices:
- the "battle trumpet" in the original becomes a "French horn"
- "no clansman would knuckle to him" is changed for "a clan never passes by but will truckle to him"...

[3] Prince Charles' mother, Maria Clementina Sobieska [K01] was the granddaughter of the famous king John Sobieski of Poland. She married James Francis Stuart, the Old Pretender, on 3rd September 1719.

[4] The Queen of Spain: Elizabeth Farnese (1692 - 1766) married by proxy, in December 1714, King Philip V of Spain (who died on 9th July 1746), a weak man over whom she quickly obtained complete influence.
Soon after his return to France (1oth October 1746) Charles Edward left to Spain hoping that he would obtain from the new King Ferdinand VI (Philip's son by his first wife) the support denied to him in France. He also intended to explore the possibility of marriage with one of the Spanish King's sisters. He managed to see King Ferdinand and the Queen who asked him, worried about English reactions, to go back to where he had come from. The Prince arrived back in Paris on 24th March 1747. It may be inferred the the song was composed during his stay in Spain.

[5] Harly her son: Elizabeth had a son of her own, Charles (1716-1788) who was acknowleged by the powers in the Treaty of Vienna as the Duke of Parma in 1731 and in 1738, he became King of the two Sicilies.
He was to abdicate his Italian thrones on succreeding to the Spanish throne as King Charles III of Spain in 1759.

[6] The gallant son of Flander: On 7th December 1745 Lord John Drummond (see [9]), brother of the Duke of Perth, landed at Montrose, coming from France, with a brigade including, among others two squadrons from the cavalry regiment Fitz-james. François de Fitz-james (1709-1764) was the son of the 1st Duke of Berwick, James FitzJames (1670-1734) referred to in the song [G18] . The latter was an illegitimate son of King James II, hence his name (fitz=fils=son). He had fought at the battles of Steinkirk and Landen and was killed at the siege of Philippsburg.

[7] Duc de Richelieu (1696-1788): Louis François Armand, great grand nephew to the famous cardinal, ought to have sailed to England with 10.000 men by the end of 1745, but he did not. See the Manifesto composed for him by Voltaire on page [O16] .

[8] Colonel Gordon O'Neill: his regiment of Derry and Tyrone men had fought at Aughrim and Limerick (1691). At Aughrim, he was found on the battlefield wounded, stripped and left for dead then rescued by relatives, serving under the enemy Williamite General Ginkel. After the war 409 rank and file men from his regiment elected to serve the French King. It was the fist Regiment of "Wild Geese". In the reorganizing of the Jacobite army O'Neill's as well as all the other regiments were disbanded. O'Neill himself became the Colonel of the Charlemont regiment of Foot. Apparently he was still alive in 1747. See page [C04] .

[9] Lord John Drummond: his brother of James Drummond, 3rd Duke of Perth, one of Prince Charles Edward Stuart's closest commanders, who was shot at Culloden, died leter of his wounds and was buried at sea. John went into exile in France.

[10] Louis le Grand: French king Louis XIV was a staunch ally of his cousin King James II/VII (unlike Louis XV).

[11] Prussia's king and Bohemia's Moll: apparently refer to the revendications set forth by Frederick II of Prussia and by Maria-Theresa of Austria during the War of Austrian Succession which the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle ended in october 1748.

The other names Cope (Preston Pans), Hawley (Falkirk), Noll (Cromwell), Coloden (Culloden) certainly require no further explanation.

Willy and George who shall "be discharged" are, of course, King George II and his second son, William Duke of Cumberland, the butcher of Culloden.
